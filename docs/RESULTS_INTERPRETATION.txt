RESULTS INTERPRETATION

1. Urban Green Infrastructure Structure

The final reporting area covers 38.8872 km². Built-up land is the dominant class, occupying 21.6040 km² or 55.56% of the study area. Dense Urban Forest covers 7.5759 km² (19.48%), Healthy Green Space 6.2446 km² (16.06%), Urban Woodland 2.4494 km² (6.30%), Water 0.8412 km² (2.16%), Stressed Green Space 0.1626 km² (0.42%) and Bare Surface 0.0095 km² (0.02%).

2. Ecosystem-Service Capacity

Mean ecosystem-service capacity across the reporting area is 41.54/100. Dense Urban Forest records the highest mean capacity at 98.82, followed by Urban Woodland at 77.81 and Healthy Green Space at 70.43. Built-up land records a mean capacity of 8.00.

3. Ecosystem-Service Demand

Mean ecosystem-service demand is 0.5768. Built-up land records the highest class-level mean demand at 0.7339. Healthy Green Space also shows relatively high mean demand at 0.6168. Capacity and demand are negatively associated (Spearman rho = -0.6043), indicating that stronger demand is often concentrated where local ecosystem-service capacity is weaker.

4. Ecosystem-Service Deficit

Mean ecosystem-service deficit is 0.4110. Demand is strongly and positively related to deficit (Spearman rho = 0.8715), while capacity is strongly and negatively related to deficit (rho = -0.8764). Built-up areas record a mean deficit of 0.6751, substantially higher than Dense Urban Forest (0.0031), Urban Woodland (0.0597) and Healthy Green Space (0.1818).

5. Planning-Priority Distribution

Very Low priority covers 7.7516 km², Low 7.7494 km², Moderate 7.7633 km², High 7.8075 km² and Very High 7.8154 km². Because quintiles were used, each class occupies approximately one-fifth of the reporting area. High and Very High classes together cover approximately 15.6229 km² or 40.17%.

6. Relationship Between UGI Classes and Planning Priority

The strongest planning result is the concentration of High and Very High priority within built-up land. Approximately 72.30% of built-up land falls within these two classes, compared with only 0.02% of non-built land. Deficit and priority are strongly associated (Spearman rho = 0.9802), as expected because priority is derived from the continuous deficit ranking.

The results show a clear spatial mismatch between ecological capacity and urban demand. High-capacity forest and woodland generally experience low deficits, while the built-up fabric contains the strongest concentration of capacity-demand imbalance.
